# Episode Guide
Each episode centers on a single retrieval operation. The works are fictional but historically grounded. The cities are real. The legal and institutional frameworks described — auction practices, museum policies, insurance structures, provenance documentation — are accurate.

##  Episode 1  La Fille au Châle Rouge
The Painting: "La Fille au Châle Rouge" (Girl with a Red Shawl), oil on canvas, attrib. to a minor Post-Impressionist, Paris, c.1921

### Provenance
Acquired by a Paris dealer from a Jewish family in 1941 under occupation-era duress — the family required an exit visa the dealer could facilitate. The painting has passed through three subsequent sales, each with documentation that carefully does not mention 1941. It currently hangs in the drawing room of an English collector in Mayfair who purchased it at a Geneva auction in 2009. The seller's provenance statement covered 1945 to present.

### Cold Open
A private viewing at a London gallery. Claire is there professionally — she has been asked to look at something unrelated. She stops in front of a photograph in a catalogue. The painting in the photograph is not in this gallery. She knows where it is. She has known for two years. The camera holds on her face for a moment before the scene cuts to black.

### The Story
We roll back to The French House and the night Sara said 'just nick it.' The ensemble assembles. The thought experiment becomes a plan. William researches the collector. Layla maps the security system. Anton inspects the painting on the pretext of an insurance valuation — and confirms what Claire already knew: the work is everything she said it was, and the documentation is a fiction. The operation is set for the night before the painting is to be transported to the collector's second home in Portugal.

### The Heist & Complication
The transport timing changes without notice. They have six hours less than planned. Sara compresses the schedule in a way that is not comfortable. What goes wrong is small and human: a panic response, a light left on, a neighbour who is home when she should not be. They solve it. The painting goes to a Paris address. A letter goes to a notaire. The forgery hangs in Mayfair and will hang there until, presumably, someone looks very closely. At the end of the episode, the collector stands in front of it and says something admiring. He is admiring Anton's work. He does not know this.

### Frame Narrative Fragment
William: *'The first one was supposed to be the proof of concept. The thing that told us whether we were capable of doing this at all.' [Pause.] 'We thought it went perfectly. That was our first mistake.'*

### What the Audience Learns
The Washington Principles: what they are, what they promised, why they failed. How provenance documentation is constructed and where the gaps are. What 'acquired under duress' means legally and why it is so difficult to prove across eighty years of subsequent sales.

##  Episode 2  Studie in Blau
The Painting: "Studie in Blau" (Study in Blue), gouache on paper, Czech modernist, Prague, c.1935

### Provenance
Confiscated from a Jewish collector in Prague following the German occupation of Czechoslovakia in 1938. The collector was deported in 1942. The work passed through the German state apparatus into a private collection in Munich, then to an Austrian museum in 1971 via a donation whose origins were not examined. The museum is a signatory to the Washington Principles. The work is on display.

### Cold Open
Anton, alone, in the conservation studio of the museum in Vienna. He is there legitimately — a contracted conservation assessment. He is standing in front of a different work entirely. But his eyes are somewhere else. A guard walks through. Anton does not move.

### The Story
The episode explores what it means to steal from a public institution — the moral and operational differences from a private collector. The gang is less comfortable. Claire is the most conflicted; this is exactly the kind of case the Washington Principles were supposed to handle. She wrote about it in her paper. The museum's position, when Layla finds the internal communications, is that the provenance is 'under review.' It has been under review for eleven years. Anton has a specific and unspoken relationship to this work — his grandfather's family lost things in the same period, different country. He does not say this to the group.

### The Heist & Complication
The complication is the museum's own conservation process: the work is scheduled for treatment, which means it will be in Anton's hands legitimately for two days. This is almost too convenient, which is the problem — it will look, afterward, like an inside job, which it is. They need the disappearance to look like something else entirely. Layla engineers a data incident that redirects the museum's attention at the critical moment. The forgery Anton leaves is arguably better than the original. He does not consider this ironic.

### Frame Narrative Fragment
William: *'Anton never talked about his family. Not once, across all of it. I found out afterward, from someone else. I've thought about that quite a lot — whether it changed anything. It didn't. But it should have meant something that I didn't know.'*

### What the Audience Learns
How public institutions handle provenance disputes. The gap between signing international agreements and implementing them. What conservation treatment involves, and why access to a work during treatment is the most intimate contact a non-owner can have with a painting.

## Episode 3  Femme à la Fenêtre, Soir
The Painting: "Femme à la Fenêtre, Soir" (Woman at the Window, Evening), oil on canvas, French, c.1938

### Provenance
Sold by the artist herself — a French Jewish painter — to a dealer in 1940 for a fraction of its value, in exchange for assistance that was promised and not provided. The artist died in 1943. The dealer's estate sold it in 1967. It is now held in a private collection in Geneva — a foundation, technically, with art-historical significance claimed as its mandate.

### Cold Open
Claire and William at a Geneva reception. They are there as themselves — she has been invited to speak at a symposium; he is her guest. The painting is on the wall. They stand in front of it talking about something else entirely. The host approaches and says something about the foundation's commitment to public access.

### The Story
Geneva introduces the financial architecture of the art world — foundations, holding structures, the specific ways in which private ownership is laundered into institutional-sounding respectability. William is more at home here than anywhere else in the series; this is adjacent to his professional world. He is also more uncomfortable, for the same reason. The artist's estate has a living heir — a grandniece in Lyon — who does not know the work exists and whose legal claim would be clear if she could prove the 1940 sale was made under duress.

### The Heist & Complication
The operation runs through the symposium itself. Claire, on stage, delivering a paper, is also running a clock. The complication is that the foundation's director has become genuinely interested in Claire and invites her to a private viewing the following morning — the very morning they need her to be elsewhere. She attends the viewing. She sees things. She tells the others. They adjust. The timing is the tightest yet. Sara does not sleep.

### Frame Narrative Fragment
William: *'Claire gave the best paper I ever heard her give that night. Afterward I asked her how she'd managed to concentrate. She said: 'I wasn't concentrating. I was counting.' I didn't ask what.'*

### What the Audience Learns
How art foundations and holding structures work and why they are the preferred vehicle for contested collections. The specific provisions of the Washington Principles around private holdings. What happened to Jewish artists — not just collectors — under the occupation.

## Episode 4  De Tuin in Oktober
The Painting: "De Tuin in Oktober" (The Garden in October), oil on panel, Dutch Golden Age, c.1670

### Provenance
The provenance chain is long and apparently clean until 1940, when a gap of six years appears in the documentation. The work resurfaces in Amsterdam in 1946 in the possession of a Dutch collaborator's estate. It has been sold three times since, each time with documentation that presents the 1946 reappearance as a wartime recovery rather than a wartime acquisition. It currently belongs to a Rotterdam shipping dynasty.

### Cold Open
Layla, in a server room she should not be in, connecting a device to a port behind a rack, watching a timer on her phone. The door handle moves. She does not look up.

### The Story
The episode is partly Layla's story — the most sustained focus on her we have had. The Rotterdam family's digital security is serious; this is the first operation where Layla is genuinely working at the edge of her capability, and the episode earns time inside her perspective. The shipping dynasty has members who know what they have and members who do not, which creates an internal tension within the family that the gang can use.

### The Heist & Complication
The complication is that someone else is looking at the same painting — a legitimate researcher, hired by a heir of the original owner, who has independently traced the provenance gap and is close to publishing. If she publishes before the operation, the painting becomes a public controversy and the operation becomes impossible. If she publishes after the painting disappears, she becomes a suspect. They need to time this exactly. They also need to talk to her without telling her anything. Layla handles this. Her family's history of displacement gives her a specific fluency in speaking around things.

### Frame Narrative Fragment
William: *'Layla was the one who could talk to the researcher without lying to her. Not because she's a good liar — she's an excellent liar — but because she understood what the woman was actually asking. I think she recognised something. She never said what.'*

### What the Audience Learns
How provenance gaps are created, maintained, and eventually found. The specific mechanisms by which Dutch Golden Age works were looted and how many remain in Dutch institutional and private collections. The difference between a painting with a gap in its documentation and a painting with a falsified one.

## Episode 5  Bildnis Einer Unbekannten
The Painting: "Bildnis Einer Unbekannten" (Portrait of an Unknown Woman), oil on canvas, Austrian, c.1912

### Provenance
Vienna, 1938. The collection of a prominent Jewish banking family, seized under the Nuremberg Laws within weeks of the Anschluss. The portrait passed through the Führermuseum pipeline — destined for Hitler's planned museum in Linz — and was diverted in the final months of the war into a private cache. Its postwar journey is murky in ways that suggest deliberate obscuring. It is now held by a European collector with connections to several governments. He is not someone who loses things gracefully.

### Cold Open
The whole gang, in Vienna, at what appears to be a celebratory dinner. They are dressed well. There are glasses raised. Something in the body language is wrong — not between the gang, but at the edge of the frame. Someone at another table is watching.

### The Story
The fifth episode is where the series tightens. The collector is not a museum or a family with competing interests — he is a single, attentive, well-connected individual who acquired this work knowing exactly what it was and has had it secured accordingly. This is also the episode in which the wrong acquisition happens — a second work is taken by accident, or is it by design by someone in the gang acting unilaterally. The episode does not resolve this. The gang does not discuss it directly. Something has shifted.

### The Heist & Complication
The operation succeeds. The portrait reaches Vienna's Commission for Provenance Research, anonymously, with documentation. The second work is not mentioned in the operation debrief. In the frame narrative, William pauses before answering a question about Vienna. The interviewer notices. The audience does too.

### Frame Narrative Fragment
William: *'Vienna was the one I think about. Not because of what went wrong — nothing went wrong, technically. Because of what we didn't say to each other afterward. That dinner in the cold open. We were celebrating. I'm still not sure what.'*

### What the Audience Learns
The Führermuseum project and the scale of planned cultural appropriation it represented. The Austrian government's specific and complicated relationship to restitution. How provenance research commissions work and why they are chronically underfunded.

## Episode 6  O Jardim das Laranjeiras
The Painting: "O Jardim das Laranjeiras" (The Orange Grove), oil on canvas, Portuguese, c.1928

### Provenance
The painting's original owner was a Sephardic Jewish family in Lisbon with commercial connections across Europe. The work was not seized but sold in 1943 under financial pressure created by the wartime disruption of the family's business — technically a voluntary sale, practically not. Its legal status is the most ambiguous of all the works in the series. Portugal signed the Washington Principles. The painting is held by a Lisbon institution that knows the history and has taken the position that the sale was voluntary and therefore valid.

### Cold Open
William alone in the interview room. The interviewer asks: 'Why Lisbon?' William is quiet for a long moment. Then: 'Because it was the last one we thought we were going to do.'

### The Story
The final operation is the most legally and morally complex. The ambiguity of the sale — technically voluntary, practically coerced — means the gang has no clean provenance argument, only a human one. Claire takes this personally in a new way; the Marchand family's painting in the first episode was also sold under technically legal duress. The episode parallels the beginning. In the frame narrative, the interview takes a turn: a question is asked about a specific work — not the Lisbon painting, but the second work from Vienna. William goes still. He is reconstructing what the interviewer knows, what the question means, who sent them.

### The Heist & Complication
The Lisbon operation runs. Something from the Vienna episode resurfaces at exactly the wrong moment. The gang is split across two problems simultaneously. The operation succeeds and fails in different measures — the painting is returned to the family's surviving descendant, but the cost is information. William, in the interview room, understands in the middle of answering a question that he is not in a deposition. He has been telling this story to someone who needed him to tell it. He stops. He looks at the interviewer. The interviewer looks back. The recorder is still running.

### Frame Narrative Fragment
William: [long pause] *'I think we're done here.' [The interviewer does not move.] 'Unless you'd like to tell me who you're actually working for.'*

### What the Audience Learns
What 'voluntary sale under duress' means and why it is the hardest category of restitution claim. Portugal's wartime neutrality and what it meant for Jewish refugees and their assets. The Washington Principles' specific blind spots around sales that are legally clean but morally illegitimate.
