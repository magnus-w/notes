## a) The war in Ukraine
It's mentioned in the "history" part, but then it's almost never mentioned in the scenario. Only by "proxy", when Baltic and Nordic countries are worried about the Russians. Since Russia's economy is smaller than the combined Nordic economies, it's reasonable to expect that a prolonged war will weaken Russia considerably. And apart from the geopolitcal impact on the EU, for this scenario the war has a very hands on effect on AI. 

Just look at the autonomous drone swarm system SAAB built during the winter. It can either fly together with its "mother drone" - like a larger, modern Reaper - or with a manned fighter jet. Or both. The onboard, military AI models are very advanced, without using frontier LLM:s. This points to a potential development fork worth exploring further. (The aftermath of conflicts in Iran and Lebanon will similarly drive innovation—underscoring that necessity isn't the mother of invention; conflict is.)

## b) Climate Change
Again, we have alread in mid June an intense heat wave in France and other EU countries. (Even authorities in Nordic countries released a severe weather, heat warning last week.) This plus floods and shortened winter seasons, well, you're unfortunately to familiar with all the different climate impacts across the EU. Given that hyperscaling compute is straining energy production, and global AI demand in 2027 is projected to account for 4.2–6.6 trillion liters of water, the connection is clear. 

There's going to be a very real impact from scaling and then there's going to be a great public opinion problem for scalers that's separate from the labour issues: The voters are going to ask politicians if they will give their citizens water or let the scalers keep guzzling.

There's also potential upsides for this factor: The push in the Nordics for green energy and manufacturing will give Europe a competitive edge when it comes to datacenters with cheap energy in volumes from hydro/nuclear/hydrogen.  

## c) Omställningsstöd
While labour insurance is mentioned, and there's a Danish example. But the larger systems Sweden established in the 1950s aren't addressed. Where the government, the industry and the unions all deposit large chunks each month in 'omställningsfonder' - transition support funds. Currently in Sweden, workers aged 27–63 can receive 80% of their salary while pursuing education that updates their skills. 

Since the EU members are good at benchmarking each other, those working with labour in the commission are already looking adapting this systemin more countries. This will not only mitigate labour crises, but actually produce 'new talent". Sweden is historically a great example, transforming a large manufacturing workforce from the 1970s to a majority of white collar employees by the 1990s. The close collaboration between unions and industry—known as the Swedish Model—was key and could have outsized impact on the AI transition. 

## d) The Black Swan
The scenario is really engagingly narrated and using the setup of telling a fictional story from the point of view of believable characters is a great tool. What's perhaps missing, if you compare with the Oxford Scenario Model, is the two other scenarios... :-) Obviously I realize that could've made the exercise too immense, as well as the reading. But there's one thing I think could go into the ending from the 'Oxford methodology'. 

Because it's the one scenario, it becomes a bit linear and even Carolines thoughts on what should've been done during the interview are 'locked inside' this specific universe. This makes it verge on forecasting. But if we combine Taleb's "The Black Swan" with a little AI history and the military models from SAAB, what do we get? You already know all about tensorflow, transformers and the development of the LLM:s. Few people (aside from insiders at OpenAI, DeepMind, and similar organizations) predicted ChatGPT when GPT-2 was restricted in 2019. 

And what if hyperscaling reaches a point where LLM models can't improve further, regardless of available energy and water resources. So obviously we'll have another 'ChatGPT & Claude Code moment' during your timeframe. SAABs onboard models which, for obvious reasons, can't use US based LLMs, point to one trajectory: Combining well trained agents with expert systems. Or we'll see somthing completely different.

Just as people during the dot-com crash couldn't anticipate dynamic web platforms, blogs, content management systems, and social media even when they were only months away.

Europes mission, should it choose to accept it, isn't to play catch up in the hyperscaling race. It's to invent the next Black Swan of AI.
