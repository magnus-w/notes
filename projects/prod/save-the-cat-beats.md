# Save the Cat! Beat Sheet

Blake Snyder's screenwriting beat sheet (from *Save the Cat!*, 2005). Originally built for
110-page screenplays, but percentage-based so it scales to novels, TV pilots, short stories,
and narrative non-fiction. Use it as a diagnostic/checkpoint tool — not a mad-libs generator.

**Naming origin**: "save the cat" = a screenwriting principle, not a beat. Early in the story,
the protagonist should do something small and likable (e.g. save a cat) so the audience roots
for them before the plot gets complicated.

## The 15 Beats

| # | Beat | Page (of 110) | % | Function |
|---|------|---------------|---|----------|
| 1 | **Opening Image** | p.1 | ~1% | Snapshot of the "before" world. Sets tone, mood, hero's starting point. Often visually mirrored by the Final Image. |
| 2 | **Theme Stated** | p.5 | ~5% | Someone (usually not the hero) says a line hinting at the story's real lesson. Hero doesn't get it yet. |
| 3 | **Set-Up** | p.1–10 | 1–10% | Establish the hero's flawed status quo, supporting cast, what's missing in their life. Plant payoffs. |
| 4 | **Catalyst** | p.12 | ~11% | Inciting incident. Something disrupts the status quo — a call, a death, an offer, an accident. |
| 5 | **Debate** | p.12–25 | 11–25% | Hero hesitates: "Should I really do this?" Raises stakes/doubt before commitment. |
| 6 | **Break into Two** | p.25 | ~25% | Hero actively *chooses* to enter the new world/plan. A decisive turn, not something that just happens to them. |
| 7 | **B Story** | p.30 | ~30% | Secondary storyline starts — usually a relationship — often carries the thematic/emotional lesson. |
| 8 | **Fun and Games** | p.30–55 | 30–50% | The "promise of the premise." Trailer-moment stuff, exploring the concept in the new world. Often the most entertaining section. |
| 9 | **Midpoint** | p.55 | ~50% | False victory or false defeat. Stakes escalate and get more real; shift from "wandering" to a ticking clock. |
| 10 | **Bad Guys Close In** | p.55–75 | 50–75% | External pressure (antagonist) and internal pressure (doubt, team fractures) both intensify. |
| 11 | **All Is Lost** | p.75 | ~75% | Lowest point. Often includes a "whiff of death" — literal or symbolic. Opposite of the Midpoint's high. |
| 12 | **Dark Night of the Soul** | p.75–85 | 75–77% | Hero sits in the despair of the loss before finding the way forward. Reflection before resolution. |
| 13 | **Break into Three** | p.85 | ~85% | Thanks to a new idea/insight (often from the B Story), hero figures out the solution — combining Act One and Two lessons. |
| 14 | **Finale** | p.85–110 | 85–100% | Hero executes the plan, confronts antagonists, proves they've changed. Sub-beats: gather the team → storm the castle → high tower surprise → dig deep down → execution of new plan. |
| 15 | **Final Image** | p.110 | ~100% | Mirror/opposite of the Opening Image — visual proof of transformation. |

## Why It Works as a Diagnostic Tool

- **Percentage-based, not page-based** — beat 6 should land ~20–25% through *any* length story.
- **Every beat = a question to interrogate a draft with**:
  - Have I actually shown the "before" world?
  - Does my hero choose to act, or just get swept along?
  - Is there a real false-victory at the midpoint, or does tension flatline?
  - Does the Dark Night beat earn the solution, or does it come out of nowhere?

## The 10 Genres (mechanism-based, not setting-based)

A second diagnostic layer Snyder proposed — genre by *mechanism*, not by surface setting:

1. Monster in the House
2. Golden Fleece
3. Out of the Bottle
4. Dude with a Problem
5. Rites of Passage
6. Buddy Love
7. Whydunit
8. Fool Triumphant
9. Institutionalized
10. Superhero

## Common Criticism

Accused of producing formulaic, homogenized stories — if everyone hits the same beats at the
same percentages, stories can feel interchangeable. Counter-argument: it's meant as a
diagnostic tool to spot *where a draft is structurally weak*, not a template to mechanically
fill in.
