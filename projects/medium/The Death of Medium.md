## It’s not Trust, it’s Authenticity
In a recent post, Medium is heralding an intellectually weak and faulty argument, implicitly to post-rationalize being soft on A.I.. It goes like this: We, the subscription payers and readers, do not care if a text is written by A.I. or not, we care if we can trust the text. 

This is faulty on at least three levels: 

1. This is not based on any evidence or research, but rather a personal opinion. An assumption. How many subscribers agree is pure guesswork.
2. It's a backwards flowing non sequitur. Without a logical connection, it tries to establish a parallel dimension to "Written by A.I."/"Not written by A.I.", i.e. "Can be trusted"/"Cannot be trusted". Implying that an A.I. created text that can be trusted is OK. 
3. How could an text generated by A.I. ever be "trusted"? OpenAI and Anthropic scraped and stole content from writers across the web, and Medium is one of the platforms pillaged right after X and YouTube. Without Medium trying to stop it, trying to protect its writers, or any renumeration for the IP. (See "A.I. Empire" by XXX XXX.) Why would anyone trust a thief caught in the act? (with his pants down)

We can also disprove the argument in a purely textbased context. Imagine a short story written in the perspective on an untrustworthy narrator. The only thing a reader would like to trust here is that the story is written by a human, beacuse otherwise it's not worth reading.

Why not? See below. 

But TL;DR, because as readers we want authenticity. (And also, see what I did there? I was writing for the Medium machine, where paragraphs consisting of a single sentence are flooding the platform.)

## Writing for the Machine 
The comparision with writing for SEM isn't only misplaced, but also misinformed. Writers have had 'formats' to comply with as long as they have been earning their keep as wordsmiths. One of the funniest examples of writing for a machine is when journalism changed because of the combination between the telegraph and the Civil War. 

The telegraph lines would get pulled down by war incidents, and writers filing stories learnt to change the structure from a three act drama to leading with the most important stuff and then having the rest in a receeding order of importance. (Hence 'a lede'.) So they would make sure that the important infomation got through in case the lines got cut. 

This way of writing persisted even to digital typesetting, so layout could cut the galleys from the back and up if something big happened that required a change in the space a certain story could take up on the page, without asking editors what to cut.

Placing keywords in a text to get better ranking results was discouraged by Google 15 years ago, and if you still did it, you got penalized with a lower ranking or none at all. They realized that it led to bad reading and changed the ranking algorithm to a semantic approach. The text had to make sense to a human reader.

So it's actually an example of the opposite to letting in A.I. genereated text.

## Exposing the Intent
One of the easiest ways to meassure the authenticity of a text, and the inherent value it has for potential readers, is to look at the intent. Why was it created? 

If someone used A.I. to 'cut corners'; to be able to produce more content faster, in order to increase revenue, the value to the reader is of course rapidly approaching zero. 

Because the A.I. generated content is not there to be read, it's there to 'exist', to be part of a feed, to be recommended to readers, to be 'curated'and to be monetized through advertising.

## Without Authenticity, Curation is Dishonest
Anyone who has seen what 'likes' and 'recommendations' have done to our feeds will have a more sober view than Medium seems to have. It doesn't matter that you try to rebrand it as 'curation', 

One of the best examples of why we have already moved away from the 'curation era' is the infamous Spotify playlists, that were revealed to consist of a large amount of fake artists, reducing the amount of royalties Spotify had to pay and the trust in the platform. (There's a logical connection between trust and another factor.)

Disingenuously, their 'curators' would make sure the playlist led with real, famous artists, but then fill it up with fake tracks. Trust in the platform sunk so low, that they had to introduce the concept of 'verified artists', which only goes to show that there we unverfied ones. 

That verification, that badge of authenticity, shows how important real authorship is. But it's also soon becoming law.

Two years ago, Leica introduced the first camera with the abilty to place a digital watermark of authenticity on your image. This is not surprising coming from a maker of cameras prefered by photojournalists and documentarians, in an age of A.I. slop. 

But it's also an example of foresight. In the E.U., the updated Data Protection Act and the upcoming A.I. Act will make it mandatory to clearly mark content generated by A.I. To not comply will be fined at the same huge scale as GDPR. In other words, if Medium (and/or its writers) doesn't mark content generated by A.I., the platform cannot be used in Europe. 

## Economy is the Bottom Line
So apart from what we've discussed so far, why is it not worth it to read A.I. generated content on Medium, making authenticity such an important feature?

Well, it all comes down to the subscription model. But not only that of Medium, but that of the LLM:s as well. 

If I have a subscription to Claude or ChatGPT, or even the free version, why would I pay to read what someone else has prompted the same model to generate?

I can just as easily prompt the model myself. 

And here's the rub with the good/bad A.I. writing argument: The better LLM:s get at writing, the more likely people are to prompt them themselves, instead of reading what others have generated through prompting.

Because why would they?
